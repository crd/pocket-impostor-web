Pocket Impostor

Privacy

Last updated 16 August 2026

Pocket Impostor has no accounts, no ads, and no trackers. Nothing you type into it ever leaves your device.

The short version. Player names, your own words and your scores stay on your phone — always. Two things are sent: how the game was played, so we can work out which parts people enjoy and make it better, and what went wrong when it breaks, so we can fix it. Neither contains anything you typed, anything anyone said, or any of the secret words.

What never leaves your device

Player names, any words you enter yourself, your scores, and your game settings are stored locally using the operating system's own storage. They are not uploaded, not backed up to us, and not shared. Deleting the app deletes all of it.

There is no account to create and no way to sign in, so there is nothing to connect any of this to a person even in principle.

How the game was played

So we can make it more fun

This is a small game made by one person, and the honest reason this data exists is that without it we are guessing. Whether a mode is enjoyable or merely confusing looks the same from the outside — unless you can see that people start a round of it and quietly stop playing. The same goes for how many rounds a group actually wants, and whether the impostor wins too often to be fair or too rarely to be exciting.

What is recorded What it tells us
A game started, and its settings Which modes and options people actually choose
How many people were playing Whether the rules hold up at three players or eight
How a round ended — caught, or not Whether the game is balanced
Rounds played, and how long it took Whether a game runs to a satisfying finish or fizzles out
That a round was played with your own words Whether that feature is worth developing further
Which built-in word pair was dealt, as a number Which pairs make a good round and which are duds

Every one of those is a number or a fixed setting. They are structurally incapable of holding a player's name, the words you typed, or who the impostor was — that is not a promise to be careful, it is a property of how the app is built, and an automated test plays a full game before every release to prove nothing readable can escape.

The last row is worth being precise about. The built-in word list ships inside the app, so we record which of its pairs a round used — as a number, never the word itself. Some pairs make a round that is over in seconds and some make one nobody can solve, and there is no way to find and fix those without knowing which was played.

Words you type yourself are never identified, not even as a number. That row is simply blank for them. Your own words are yours; the built-in list is already public.

What kind of device it was

So we can fix faults we cannot reproduce

A bug report of "it broke" is nearly unfixable. The same bug labelled "on Android, version 1.0.1, tablet-sized screen" usually points straight at the cause. Four coarse facts travel with the records above:

  • Platform — iPhone, Android, or the web
  • Which version of the game you are running
  • The major version of your operating system — "18", not "18.3.1"
  • Screen size category — phone, tablet, or desktop

That is the complete list, and where it stops is deliberate. Not your device model, not your exact system version, not your time zone, not your screen dimensions, not your language, and not your location or IP address. Each of those would narrow things down towards one particular machine, and none of them would help fix a single bug that the four above cannot.

One random number

So the counts mean something

The first time you open the game, it generates a random number and keeps it on your device. It is sent with everything described above.

Without it the data is close to useless in two specific ways. Ten games might be one group who loved it or ten groups who each tried it once and left — opposite conclusions, and no way to tell them apart. And a fault reported three hundred times might be one unlucky person stuck in a loop or three hundred people hitting it once, which need entirely different fixes.

It is a number and nothing more. Not linked to a name, an email, an account, or an advertising identifier. Not shared with anyone. It cannot be connected to anything outside this game — including by us. Nobody can look up who it belongs to, and we have no way to find out.

To reset it: delete and reinstall the app, or clear this site's data in your browser. A new number is generated, and the old one cannot be recovered by anybody, ourselves included.

No cookies are used on this website.

When it breaks

So it stops breaking

If the app crashes, it sends a report of the fault. This is handled by Sentry and is configured to carry as little as it can while still being useful:

  • The technical detail of the fault — where in the code it happened.
  • A list of which screens you had passed through, such as "setup, reveal, discussion". Crash reporting normally records every button you tapped and everything the app logged; that is switched off here specifically because a secret word could travel out inside it. Screen names describe where a fault happened without describing anything you saw.
  • The random number above, and no other identifier.
  • No name, email, or account — there are none to send. No IP address is recorded.

What we never do

  • No advertising, and no advertising identifiers.
  • No tracking you across other apps or websites.
  • No selling or sharing your data with anyone.
  • No cookies.
  • No access to your microphone, camera, contacts, photos, or location.

Children

The game suits a general audience and collects nothing that could identify anyone, of any age. It asks nobody for personal information.

Changes

If this policy changes, the date at the top changes with it. If the app ever started collecting something genuinely new, that would be announced in the app itself rather than quietly edited in here.
